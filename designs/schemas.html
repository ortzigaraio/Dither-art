Schemas of Diffusion

How a painting becomes a probability field — notes on error, threshold and the uncertainty of every pixel.

Abstract

A dithered image is a map of decisions made under uncertainty. For each pixel the algorithm must choose a single ink from a tiny palette, and the difference between what it wanted and what it could have — the error — is redistributed to the pixels that have not yet been decided. We visualise this process on a small corpus of paintings, compare four families of screens, and argue that the residual pattern is itself a legible diagram of the image.

1. The kernel

Floyd and Steinberg’s kernel1 distributes the quantisation error of a pixel to four unvisited neighbours with the weights 7, 3, 5 and 1 sixteenths. The weights sum to one: no light is lost, it is only displaced. Over the whole image the displacement becomes a texture, and the texture is what we read as grey.

The ordered alternative, Bayer’s threshold matrix2, removes the dependency between pixels entirely. Each position on an 8 × 8 tile is given a fixed threshold, and the image is compared against that tile repeated to infinity. It is parallel, deterministic and — to a trained eye — unmistakably mechanical.

1 R. W. Floyd & L. Steinberg, “An adaptive algorithm for spatial greyscale”, Proc. SID 17, 1976.

2 B. E. Bayer, “An optimum method for two-level rendition of continuous-tone pictures”, IEEE ICC, 1973.

Note — in the browser both run per frame on 1.6 Mpx in < 40 ms (FS, worker) and < 2 ms (Bayer, fragment shader).

3. Reading the residue

If we subtract the dithered image from the original we are left with the residue: the exact shape of the algorithm’s doubt. In flat areas it is a fine, even noise; at edges it organises itself into worms and ridges that follow the contours of the painting. The residue is not noise in the statistical sense. It is a second drawing, made of everything the first one could not say.

We propose to treat this residue as an output in its own right — a diagram of uncertainty that can be exported, plotted and read alongside the image it came from.
